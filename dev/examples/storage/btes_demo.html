Borehole Thermal Energy Storage (BTES) ​

Storage   BTES  

This example demonstrates how to set up and simulate seasonal Borehole Thermal Energy Storage (BTES) using Fimbul, and compares three patterns for placing the boreholes. The BTES system consists of multiple boreholes coupled in series and parallel.

julia
using Jutul, JutulDarcy
using Fimbul
using HYPRE
using GLMakie

Set up simulation cases ​

We create BTES systems with 40 wells that extend to 100 m depth, divided into 5 sectors of 8 wells each. The wells are placed in a sunflower, a circular and a rectangular (8 x 5) pattern, all with 5 m between neighboring wells. The systems operate on a seasonal cycle: wells are charged during summer months (April to September) with hot water at 90°C, and discharged during winter months (October to March) with cold water at 10°C. This seasonal operation is simulated over a 4-year period to study the thermal energy storage performance.

The wells of the sunflower pattern are grouped into sectors along its spiral spines (sector_grouping = :spiral, the default). For the circular pattern, the wells are grouped into sectors from the center outwards (sector_grouping = :sequential), instead of into circle sectors, which is the default. For the rectangular pattern, the sectors are blocks of 4 x 2 wells, with the last sector in the top row (sector_grouping = :cartesian).

Adjusting the well fields

The size of the fields is set by num_wells and num_sectors. Instead of the number of wells, the spacing between them can be given (well_spacing), and the size of the field can be set with radius (sunflower and circular) or extent = (Lx, Ly) (rectangular), see btes. For the rectangular pattern, the blocks that make up the sectors are as square as possible, or set by sector_dims = (nx, ny); for example, 45 wells in 5 sectors gives four blocks of 3 x 3 wells and a last sector of 9 wells in the two top rows. The sunflower sectors are made of whole spines when the number of sectors is 2, 3, 5 or 8; otherwise, a few outer wells are moved between neighboring sectors to even them out.

The field in this example is kept small so that it runs quickly. The differences between the patterns grow with the number of wells, so increase num_wells (e.g. to 100) to see larger differences, at the cost of longer simulations.

Visualize the BTES layouts ​

During charging, water is injected at 0.5 l/s into the first well of each sector and flows sequentially through all wells in series to the last well. During discharging, the flow is reversed (reversed_discharge = true), with water flowing from the last well back to the first. Each sector is modelled as a single closed-loop well holding its boreholes in series, which is much faster to simulate than one well per borehole (well_grouping = :borehole).

For the sunflower pattern, the sectors are bundles of adjacent spiral spines (see below), with the flow running from the center outwards. With 40 wells in 5 sectors, each sector is a single spine of the 5-spine family. For the circular pattern, the wells are numbered from the center outwards, counter-clockwise on each ring, and the sectors are consecutive groups in this order. For the rectangular pattern, the sectors are blocks of 4 x 2 wells, tiling the field from the bottom up, and the wells in the top row form the last sector. The flow runs through each sector column by column.

The structure underlying each pattern is shown in light gray:

  • Sunflower: well n sits at radius R(n−1/2)/(N−1/2) and angle nΔθ, where Δθ=2π/φ2 and φ is the golden ratio. Wells F apart in this order, for consecutive Fibonacci numbers F, line up along spirals, the "spines" of the sunflower.

  • Circular: a center well surrounded by concentric rings, one well spacing apart.

  • Rectangular: the Cartesian grid on which the wells are placed.

We plot the well positions of each pattern with plot_btes_field!, which colors the wells by sector and connects the wells of each sector in the order the fluid passes them during charging. The first well of each sector is marked with a larger symbol.

Set up reservoir simulators ​

We configure the simulator with specific tolerances and timestep controls to handle the challenging thermal transients in the BTES system. We also store the states of all ministeps (output_substates = true), which we use to compute the energy exchanged with the ground accurately.

The transition from charging to discharging creates a thermal shock that is numerically challenging for the nonlinear solver. We use a specialized timestep selector that reduces the timestep to 5 seconds during control changes to maintain numerical stability and convergence.

julia
function setup_btes_simulator(case)
    simulator, config = setup_reservoir_simulator(
        case;
        tol_cnv = 1.0e-2,
        tol_mb = 1.0e-6,
        tol_cnve_well = 1.0e-2,
        tol_cnv_well = 1.0e-2,
        timesteps = :auto,
        initial_dt = 5.0,
        target_its = 10,
        relaxation = true,
        output_substates = true,
    )
    sel = JutulDarcy.ControlChangeTimestepSelector(
        case.model, 0.0, convert_to_si(5.0, :second)
    )
    push!(config[:timestep_selectors], sel)
    config[:timestep_max_decrease] = 1.0e-6
    return simulator, config
end

Since we stored the ministep states, result.states holds the reservoir state after every ministep, while result.result.states holds the full state after each report step. Below, we use the latter whenever we need the state at a given report step.

Charging and discharging steps are found from the injection temperature. The sector wells are injected into at their supply part during charging, and at their return part during discharging, since reversed_discharge = true. All patterns share the same schedule.

julia
T_ch = convert_to_si(90.0, :Celsius)
T_dch = convert_to_si(10.0, :Celsius)
function is_injecting(forces, T_inj)
    return any(
        c -> c isa InjectorControl && c.temperature == T_inj,
        values(forces[:Facility].control)
    )
end
schedule = cases[patterns[1]].forces
is_charge = [is_injecting(f, T_ch) for f in schedule]
is_discharge = [is_injecting(f, T_dch) for f in schedule];

Contiguous ranges of steps where mask holds, one per cycle. The state after a step is the state at its end, so the last step of each range gives the state at the end of the period.

julia
function step_ranges(mask)
    starts = findall(i -> mask[i] && (i == 1 || !mask[i - 1]), eachindex(mask))
    stops = findall(i -> mask[i] && (i == length(mask) || !mask[i + 1]), eachindex(mask))
    return [a:b for (a, b) in zip(starts, stops)]
end
charge_ranges = step_ranges(is_charge)
discharge_ranges = step_ranges(is_discharge);

Temperature evolution within the boreholes ​

Understanding the temperature profiles within individual boreholes is crucial for BTES system design. We analyze how temperature varies with depth along the wellbore during both charging and discharging phases. This information can help determine optimal well depth, number of wells needed, and the most effective series/parallel coupling configuration for a given thermal storage capacity requirement.

To this end, we define a utility function to plot temperature evolution in a BTES sector. This function visualizes temperature as a function of well depth for all wells in a given sector at specified timesteps. Each sector is a single well, and the borehole field gives the borehole of each well node.

julia
function plot_btes_temperature(ax, case, result, sector, timesteps)
    colors = cgrad(:ice, length(timesteps), categorical = true)
    msh = physical_representation(reservoir_model(case.model).data_domain)
    geo = tpfv_geometry(msh)
    time = convert_from_si.(cumsum(case.dt), :day)
    well = only(case.input_data[:sectors][Symbol("S$sector")])
    d = case.model.models[well].data_domain
    borehole, tag = d[:borehole, Cells()], d[:tag, Cells()]
    for (sno, step) in enumerate(timesteps)
        label = String("$(round((time[step]), digits = 1)) days")
        T = result.result.states[step][well][:Temperature]
        T = convert_from_si.(T, :Celsius)
        for b in 1:maximum(borehole)
            lbl = b == 1 ? label : nothing
            nodes = findall(
                i -> borehole[i] == b &&
                    tag[i] in (:pipe_left, :pipe_right), eachindex(borehole)
            )
            plot_mswell_values!(
                ax, case.model, well, T;
                nodes = nodes, geo = geo, color = colors[sno], linewidth = 6, label = lbl
            )
        end
    end
    return
end;

Charged and discharged energy ​

Finally, we compare how much energy each pattern stores and recovers. With only 40 wells, the differences between the patterns are small; they become larger for fields with more wells. The net energy exchanged with the ground over a ministep of length Δt is Δt∑wqwhw, where qw is the surface mass rate of well part w (positive for injection) and hw its specific enthalpy at the surface. Summing over the charging steps of a cycle gives the charged energy, and summing over the discharging steps gives the discharged energy (with opposite sign). The ratio of the two is the recovery factor of the cycle.

The substates hold the ministeps of each report step but the last, whose state is the report state itself.

julia
function exchanged_energy(result, steps)
    E = 0.0
    for step in steps
        report_state = result.result.states[step]
        ministep_states = [report_state[:substates]..., report_state]
        dt = [m[:dt] for m in result.result.reports[step][:ministeps] if m[:success]]
        @assert length(ministep_states) == length(dt)
        for (state, Δt) in zip(ministep_states, dt)
            f = state[:Facility]
            E += Δt * sum(f[:TotalSurfaceMassRate] .* f[:SurfaceEnthalpy])
        end
    end
    return E
end

MWh = si_unit(:mega) * si_unit(:watt) * si_unit(:hour)
E_charged = Dict(
    p =>
        [exchanged_energy(results[p], r) for r in charge_ranges] ./ MWh
        for p in patterns
)
E_discharged = Dict(
    p =>
        [-exchanged_energy(results[p], r) for r in discharge_ranges] ./ MWh
        for p in patterns
